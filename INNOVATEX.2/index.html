LEAD BRIEFING / AJNAR RIVER 2026

One student started. The river changed.

The left half of the picture is what the Ajnar looked like when the bed was a dump: bottles, bags, offerings and sludge stacked between the concrete pillars. The right half is after months of lifting that load by hand. Same place. Same person. Different river.

Surendra Singh Choudhary — a BSc student from Biaora, Rajgarh, known online as Bittu Tabahi — started on Republic Day, 26 January 2026. The stretch sat beside a temple he passed often. A few friends came once. The mud, smell and size of the pile sent them home. He kept a short daily window, about 3pm to 6pm, with bags he paid for himself. Reports put his own spend near ₹30,000.

Locals called the work crazy and nicknamed him a drain-cleaner. The water was filthy and the hours were hard on his hands. He still posted the change. When the clips travelled, Anand Mahindra defended the effort, Madhya Pradesh’s chief minister met him, Biaora’s municipality sent machines, and Boyan Slat of The Ocean Cleanup wrote a public invite to join the team.

Drag the white line. Left is the waste bed. Right is open channel. That is the whole argument of this page in one frame: plastic does not vanish — someone has to take it out before it rides a drain to the sea.

06 / SOLUTIONS

STOP IT.
THEN CLEAN IT.

Prevention keeps waste out of drains, rivers and the sea. Purification is what a city plant (or a careful home method) does after water is already dirty. Both matter. This is a school briefing, not a lab manual.

How to prevent water pollution

Land → drain → river → sea
01 / COLLECT

Bin it before the rain.

Most ocean plastic starts as street waste. Regular collection, covered bins and no dumping in nallahs stops the monsoon from becoming a conveyor belt.

02 / BLOCK THE PATH

Guard drains and rivers.

Drain screens, trash booms and river barriers catch bottles and bags after they blow off the street — but before they reach the beach.

03 / CUT THE SOURCE

Use less throwaway plastic.

Refill bottles, carry a bag, refuse extra packaging. Cities also use bottle-return schemes and producer rules so less single-use waste is made.

04 / TREAT SEWAGE

Don’t send dirty water raw.

Toilets and sinks should reach a treatment plant. Untreated sewage raises bacteria and BOD in coastal water. Fixing leaks and plants protects swimmers and fish.

05 / KEEP CHEMICALS IN

Paint, oil and cleaners stay off the street.

Household chemicals poured into a drain become river and sea pollution. Take them to a proper collection point. Farms and factories need the same rule.

06 / WATCH THE WET MONTHS

Monsoon is the danger window.

Litter counts jump when streets flood. Extra sweeping before rain, and boom checks during rain, cut the spike this page keeps showing in Mumbai reports.

How dirty water is cleaned

City plant + safe home steps
ScreenBars and grit chambers pull out leaves, plastic and sand so pumps do not clog.
Settle + filterHeavy mud sinks. Sand or carbon beds catch smaller bits still floating.
DisinfectCity plants use controlled treatment so germs cannot grow in the pipes. This is done by trained staff.
Home boilIf a notice says “boil water,” bring it to a rolling boil, then cool it covered. Use a clean pot.

07 / METHOD

MAKE THE DATA
EASY TO TRUST.

For a competition jury, clarity about the data is as important as the interface. This panel explains exactly what the main number means — and what it does not mean.

What is measured?

This 2026 dashboard shows each country’s share of global macroplastic emissions to the environment, using the latest complete published national inventory (Cottom et al. 2024, Our World in Data update January 2026). Earth Action’s 2026 Plastic Overshoot estimate puts mismanaged environmental leakage at about 72 million tonnes this year. Annual ocean input is still estimated at 1–2 million tonnes. These are models, not live beach sensors.

How should it be used?

Use the values to identify broad geographic signals and then investigate local waste systems, river basins, drainage, collection coverage and coastal conditions before designing an intervention.

2026 briefing notes: Earth Action estimates about 227 million tonnes of packaging, textile and household plastic waste and about 72 million tonnes mismanaged into the environment in 2026. The country ranking still uses the latest complete scientific national inventory (Cottom et al. 2024 / OWID January 2026) because no full official 2026 country-by-country ocean table has been published. Ocean-input reviews still put sea leakage around 1–2 million tonnes a year.
